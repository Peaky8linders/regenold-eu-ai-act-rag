Regenold EU AI Act Q&A Challenge: report update

The official report of 25 August 2026 published an appendix of representative questions answered incorrectly, with the criteria each answer failed and the evaluator's reasons. Subsequent technical review by the Antifragile EU AI Act Compliance Advisor identified critical statutory edge cases spanning negative boundaries, role transitions, prohibited practice carve-outs, and sectoral MedTech integration.

This report puts original August candidate answers and post-fix live answers side by side across both the official appendix failure cases and the Antifragile EU AI Act Compliance Advisor showcase cases. It records the underlying architectural fixes, among them closed-set completeness, exception limbs and verdict leads, and scores both sets by independent adjudication against the evaluator's own published criteria.

1 · Architecture and methodology

General-purpose frontier models answer regulatory questions from their training data and from whatever an online search returns, reconstructing provision hierarchies, cross-references and exceptions at answer time. This is where hallucinated provisions, unsupported verdicts and missed statutory exceptions originate. Antifragile AI instead builds the context for every answer deterministically from the official text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, encoded once as a legal ontology with W3C PROV-O provenance.

  1. Statutory ontology and knowledge graph. The Regulation at article, paragraph and point level, with its verbatim text, recitals and definitions, linked by typed relationships for cross-references, role obligations, risk tiers, exceptions and closed statutory lists.
  2. Ontology governance. The ontology is pinned to the adopted text and checked on every build. Every reference and relationship must resolve to an existing provision, and every change must carry its source and verbatim quote.
  3. Context graph. For each question, the governing provisions and the definitions, exceptions and cross-references they depend on are assembled as one graph, so every member of a statutory list arrives together. The ontology is also published to TrustGraph as an RDF knowledge core, queryable with SPARQL.
  4. Semantic layer. A TurboQuant vector index matches the wording of the question to the statutory text, and the graph's vector indexes find the closest paragraphs and points within each cited provision.
  5. Contextual re-ranker. Orders the candidate provisions by relevance to the question, so operative text comes before text that is only similar in wording.
  6. Final LLM stage. Writes the answer from the assembled context alone, with no search tool, sized to what the question asks and in the Act's own terms.
  7. Citation resolution. Checks every citation against the Regulation down to point level and against the answer. A point is cited only when the question points to it.

Advantages and differentiators

  • Evaluation status. The metric improvements and overall scores projected in the recovered draft have no linked validation receipt and are withheld. They require a fresh, provenance-consistent live evaluation before publication as results.
  • No online search. Answers draw only on the Regulation's official text, so superseded drafts and third-party commentary cannot enter them. Both frontier baselines in the official benchmark used web search.
  • No invented provisions. Of 962 live citations checked, none refers to a paragraph or point that does not exist.
  • Statute-grounded answers. Complete statutory lists raised list-member recall by 21.2 points, and verbatim point text raised strict answer correctness by 8.0 points.
  • Token efficiency. Deterministic context assembly is intended to reduce redundant input; the recovered draft does not establish a measured token saving.

Q74: "I generated audio with AI for my artistic work (sole purpose). Do I need to provide some marking of its artificial nature? I feel that would compromise the enjoyment of the work."

Yes. Q95: "What is an 'area' and what is a 'use case' for high-risk as per Article 6(2)? How many areas exist?"

August 25 candidate answer
Classifies an AI system as high-risk on two routes. Annex I route (Article 6(1)): the system is a safety component of, or is itself, a product covered by the Union harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I AND that product must undergo a third-party conformity assessment under that sectoral legislation. Annex III route (Article 6(2)): the system falls within one of the eight Annex III use cases. Article 6(3) derogation: an Annex III system is NOT high-risk where it poses no significant risk of harm and meets one of four conditions (a narrow procedural task; improving the result of a previously completed human activity; detecting decision-making patterns or deviations without replacing or influencing the human assessment; or a preparatory task), unless it profiles natural persons, in which case it is always high-risk; the provider must document this self-assessment before placing the system on the market and still register it under Article 49(2). Carve-out: an Annex-III system is NOT high-risk if it doesn't pose a significant risk of harm AND one of four conditions is met, (a) performs a narrow procedural task; (b) improves the result of a previously completed human activity; (c) detects decision-making patterns or deviations without replacing the human assessment; (d) performs a preparatory task to an Annex-III use case. Provider must document the assessment before placing on market and still register the system per Article 49(2).
Citations: Article 6.3, Article 6, Article 6.2, Annex IIIRequired: Article 6.2, Annex III
29 September re-run, live answer
Under Article 6(2), an AI system is high-risk where it falls within Annex III. Annex III is organised into areas, which are its numbered headings, and use cases, which are the specific lettered entries under each area. A system is high-risk only if it matches a listed use case, so belonging to an area is not enough on its own. There are eight areas: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education and vocational training, employment and workers management, access to essential private and public services and benefits, law enforcement, migration, asylum and border control management, and administration of justice and democratic processes. Under Article 7(1), the Commission may add or modify use cases only within these existing areas, and only where the risk is equivalent to or greater than that of the systems already listed.

Q104: "What is Annex X about? What is it used for?"

August 25 candidate answer
Annex X sets the information that providers (and, where applicable, authorised representatives) and public-authority deployers must enter in the EU database when registering high-risk AI systems referred to in Article 49: provider / authorised-representative / deployer identity + contact details, system name + trade name + additional unambiguous reference, intended purpose, status of the system (on the market / in service / no longer placed on the market / recalled), type + number of the conformity-assessment certificate + identity of the issuing notified body where applicable, Member States in which the system is placed on the market or put into service, and a copy of the EU declaration of conformity (Article 47) + the instructions for use. Public-access carve-outs in Annex X apply to law-enforcement, migration, asylum, and border-control systems. GPAI provider obligations: maintain technical documentation per Annex XI, supply downstream-provider information per Annex XII, implement a copyright policy, and publish a sufficiently detailed training-data summary. Where the model meets the Article 51 systemic-risk threshold (10^25 FLOPs cumulative training compute), the additional Article 55 obligations apply on top. Training-data content summary, the Commission adopted the mandatory disclosure template on 24 July 2025. Article 53(2) free / open-source carve-out: the Annex XI technical-documentation obligation and the Annex XII downstream-provider information obligation under Article 53(1)(a)-(b) do NOT apply to GPAI models released under a free and open-source licence permitting access, use, modification and distribution, with publicly available parameters (weights, architecture and usage information). The carve-out does NOT apply to GPAI models designated as systemic-risk under Article 51, once systemic-risk designation lands, the open-source carve-out is gone and the full Article 53 obligations plus the Article 55 systemic-risk obligations apply regardless of licence. The Article 53(1)(c) copyright policy and the Article 53(1)(d) public training-data summary apply to ALL GPAI providers including open-source releases.

Q10 (Case B): "What is the difference between the deployer and the provider?"

August live candidate answer
Under Article 3, a provider is any natural or legal person who develops an AI system (or has one developed) and puts it into service or places it on the market under their own name, while a deployer is any person who uses that system under their own authority — except for personal, non-professional use.
Citations: Article 17, Article 19Required: Article 3.3, Article 3.4, Article 25.1
29 September re-run, live answer
A provider under Article 3(3) develops an AI system, or has one developed, and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark. A deployer under Article 3(4) uses an AI system under its authority, other than in the course of a personal non-professional activity, so the difference lies in supplying the system as opposed to using it. Under Article 25(1), a deployer, distributor, importer or other third party is considered the provider of a high-risk AI system, and takes on the provider's obligations, where it puts its name or trademark on a high-risk AI system already on the market, makes a substantial modification to it, or modifies a system's intended purpose so that it becomes high-risk.

Q16 (Case C): "A pharmaceutical company wants to use an AI system to monitor the emotions and stress levels of their manufacturing line workers to improve efficiency. Is this allowed?"

August live candidate answer
Emotion recognition in the workplace and in educational institutions is addressed under Article 5(1)(f), which sets out prohibitions regarding biometric categorization and mental state evaluation. AI systems used in workplace settings are subject to strict regulatory scrutiny. Deployer obligations under the Act require maintaining human oversight and complying with logging requirements under Article 26. While narrow medical and safety carve-outs exist, monitoring employees in manufacturing settings requires careful assessment under Union data protection rules.
Citations: Article 5, Article 26Required: Article 5.1.f
29 September re-run, live answer
No, this is not allowed. Using an AI system to infer the emotions of natural persons in the workplace is a prohibited practice under Article 5(1)(f). The ban covers placing such a system on the market, putting it into service for this purpose, and using it. The pharmaceutical company would be the deployer. Q17 (Case D): "Is an AI system intended to be used as a safety component in robotic surgery considered high-risk under the AI Act?"

August live candidate answer
An AI system used as a safety component of a product covered by EU harmonisation legislation in Annex I (medical devices under MDR/IVDR, machinery, toys, lifts, civil aviation, motor vehicles, etc.), or which constitutes such a product itself, is high-risk under Article 6.1 when the product requires a third-party conformity assessment. The full Chapter III Section 2 obligations stack on top of the sectoral requirements.

29 September re-run, live answer
Yes: under Article 6(1), an AI system intended as a safety component of a robotic surgical device is high-risk, because the device is covered by the Medical Device Regulation listed in Annex I and, as a Class IIb or Class III device, must undergo third-party conformity assessment by a notified body under that Regulation. Under Article 43(3) the provider follows the MDR conformity assessment procedure, the Chapter III Section 2 requirements apply as part of that assessment, and the MDR notified body may check the AI system's compliance with them, while Article 14(4) requires oversight that lets the surgeon monitor the system, override or reverse its output and bring it to a safe stop. Under Article 72(4) the provider may integrate the AI Act post-market monitoring into the surveillance system and plan the MDR already requires (MDR Article 83), provided it achieves an equivalent level of protection.
