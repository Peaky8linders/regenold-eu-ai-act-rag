2 · Remediation

The remediation was made in the system's statutory content, not in the final language model. For each of the ten question families the system now holds a pre-drafted statutory answer that states the conditions the review identified in the same sentence as the provision imposing them: Article 13(3) with its qualifiers “where applicable”, “when appropriate” and “if any”; Article 25(1) with each of its three points; Article 50 with the marking obligation, the disclosure obligation and the manner of disclosure kept distinct. These answers are served as drafted and are not rewritten by the language model, so a condition cannot be paraphrased away. The summaries of Annex III, Annex X and Articles 5(1)(f), 7, 50 and 111 used elsewhere in the system were corrected at the same time.

A second round on 23 October addressed how these answers behave when the question is phrased differently. For eight of the ten families (all but Case A and Case D), the statutory answer is no longer shortened when the question is read as a single request, and its citations are limited to the provisions it states. A separate statutory answer to Article 25(1) questions (“When does a deployer become a provider under Article 25(1)?”) now states the condition attached to each point. The Q74 answer names the medium asked about (audio, images or video), and an image question about an art project now reaches it. Wording was corrected against the Official Journal text in three answers: a self-developer is “also” the provider (Q74); Recital 83 “should” and general-purpose AI models in Article 3(3) (Q10); and the items of Article 13(3)(b), introduced by “including” with items (ii), (iii) and (vi) in full (Q45). The answers still paraphrase the Act elsewhere.

A third round of corrections, deployed on 25 and 26 October 2026, is summarised at the head of this appendix. The ten reviewed answers did not need to change. Section 5 reports the rephrased questions and section 6 what remains open.

3 · Legal basis

The challenge rules define the scope as the AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of 13 June 2024, and the system answers on the text as adopted. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (Digital Omnibus on AI), published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and in force since 27 July 2026, has since amended the Act. Five statements in the answers below differ under the amended text. The review's corrected answers likewise apply the adopted text.

Q45

Instructions for use (Article 13)

Review: wrong in part, borderline26 Oct: 3 of 3 points resolved

“Under the EU AI Act, what must a provider of a high-risk AI system supply to the deployer in the instructions for use? List the required categories of information.”

29 September answer (as reviewed)
Under Article 13(2), the provider must accompany a high-risk AI system with instructions for use in an appropriate digital or other format that are concise, complete, correct and clear to deployers. Article 13(3) requires them to cover the following: the identity and contact details of the provider and its authorised representative; the system's characteristics, capabilities and limitations of performance, including intended purpose, accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity levels, foreseeable risk circumstances, output-explanation capabilities, performance for specific persons or groups, input data specifications and information enabling output interpretation; predetermined changes; human oversight measures; computational and hardware resources, expected lifetime and maintenance measures, including software updates; and, where relevant, the mechanisms for collecting, storing and interpreting logs.
Citations: Article 13.3Expected (official appendix): Article 13.3
26 October answer (production)
Article 13(1) requires a high-risk AI system to be designed and developed so that its operation is sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret its output and use it appropriately, with an appropriate type and degree of transparency. Article 13(2) requires it to be accompanied by instructions for use, in an appropriate digital format or otherwise, containing concise, complete, correct and clear information that is relevant, accessible and comprehensible to deployers. Article 13(3) then sets the minimum content: (a) the identity and contact details of the provider and, where applicable, of its authorised representative; (b) the characteristics, capabilities and limitations of performance, including (i) the intended purpose; (ii) the level of accuracy, including its metrics, robustness and cybersecurity under Article 15 against which the system has been tested and validated and which can be expected, and any known and foreseeable circumstances that may affect that expected level; (iii) any known or foreseeable circumstance, related to the use of the system in accordance with its intended purpose or under conditions of reasonably foreseeable misuse, that may lead to risks to health and safety or fundamental rights under Article 9(2); (iv) where applicable, the technical capabilities and characteristics that provide information relevant to explain its output; (v) when appropriate, its performance regarding specific persons or groups on which it is intended to be used; (vi) when appropriate, specifications for the input data, or any other relevant information on the training, validation and testing data sets used, taking into account the intended purpose; (vii) where applicable, information enabling deployers to interpret the output and use it appropriately; (c) the changes to the system and its performance predetermined by the provider at the initial conformity assessment, if any; (d) the human oversight measures under Article 14, including the technical measures that help deployers interpret the outputs; (e) the computational and hardware resources needed, the expected lifetime and any necessary maintenance and care measures, including their frequency and software updates, to ensure the proper functioning of the system; and (f) where relevant, the mechanisms enabling deployers to collect, store and interpret the logs under Article 12. The Article 13(3) list is a minimum, and the items qualified by where applicable, when appropriate or if any are required only where that condition is met.

Q96

Annex III areas and healthcare

Review: correct overall, 2 precision points26 Oct: 2 of 2 points resolved

“Name the areas of high-risk use cases. Is healthcare decision making one of them?”

29 September answer (as reviewed)
Article 6(2) makes an AI system high-risk if it falls within a use case listed in Annex III, which groups those use cases into eight areas: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education and vocational training, employment and worker management, access to essential private and public services and benefits, law enforcement, migration, asylum and border control management, and administration of justice and democratic processes. No, healthcare decision making is not an area of its own. Some of it is covered under area 5: Annex III(5)(a) covers public-authority assessment of eligibility for essential services, including healthcare, and Annex III(5)(d) covers emergency healthcare patient triage. Clinical AI can also be high-risk under Article 6(1) through Annex I, where it is, or is a safety component of, a medical device that needs third-party conformity assessment.
Citations: Article 6, Annex III, Annex IExpected (official appendix): Annex III
26 October answer (production)
Annex III lists high-risk AI systems under eight numbered areas: biometrics, in so far as their use is permitted under relevant Union or national law; critical infrastructure; education and vocational training; employment, workers' management and access to self-employment; access to and enjoyment of essential private services and essential public services and benefits; law enforcement, in so far as their use is permitted under relevant Union or national law; migration, asylum and border control management, in so far as their use is permitted under relevant Union or national law; and administration of justice and democratic processes. Healthcare decision making is not one of them: Annex III places healthcare at point 5(a), which covers AI systems intended to be used by public authorities or on behalf of public authorities to evaluate the eligibility of natural persons for essential public assistance benefits and services, including healthcare services, and to grant, reduce, revoke or reclaim them, and at point 5(d), which covers emergency healthcare patient triage systems. Health also appears in Annex III at point 5(c), risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance, and at point 7(b), a health risk posed by a person entering a Member State, and neither of those concerns healthcare decisions. Q17

Amendment of Annex III by delegated act

Review: correct overall, 2 precision points26 Oct: 2 of 2 points resolved

“Can the European Commission amend Annex III of the EU AI Act to add or modify use-cases classified as high-risk AI systems? Under what conditions?”

29 September answer (as reviewed)
Yes. Under Article 7(1), the Commission may adopt delegated acts under Article 97 to amend Annex III by adding or modifying high-risk use-cases. Two cumulative conditions apply. First, the systems must be intended for use in one of the eight areas already listed in Annex III: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education and vocational training, employment and workers' management, essential private and public services, law enforcement, migration, asylum and border control, and administration of justice and democratic processes. Second, they must pose a risk of harm to health and safety, or an adverse impact on fundamental rights, equivalent to or greater than that of existing Annex III systems, assessed against Article 7(2) criteria such as intended purpose, autonomy and severity of harm. Under Article 97, the Commission must consult Member State experts, and the act applies only if neither the European Parliament nor the Council objects.
Citations: Annex III, Article 7.1, Article 97Expected (official appendix): Article 7.1
26 October answer (production)
Yes, but only within the limits Article 7 sets. Under Article 7(1) the Commission may adopt delegated acts in accordance with Article 97 to amend Annex III by adding or modifying use cases of high-risk AI systems where both of the following conditions are fulfilled: the AI systems are intended to be used in any of the areas already listed in Annex III, and they pose a risk of harm to health and safety, or an adverse impact on fundamental rights, that is equivalent to or greater than the risk of harm or adverse impact posed by the high-risk AI systems already referred to in Annex III. Both conditions are cumulative, so a use case outside the existing areas cannot be added by delegated act; Article 7(2) lists the criteria for assessing that risk, and Article 7(3) allows a use case to be removed from Annex III where it no longer poses a significant risk and the overall level of protection is not reduced. Before adopting the delegated act the Commission must consult experts designated by each Member State (Article 97(4)), and the act enters into force only if neither the European Parliament nor the Council objects within three months of notification, a period extended by three months at the initiative of either of them, or earlier where both inform the Commission that they will not object (Article 97(6)).

Q74

AI-generated audio in an artistic work

Review: wrong in part26 Oct: 7 of 7 points resolved

“I generated audio with AI for my artistic work (sole purpose). Do I need to provide some marking of its artificial nature? I feel that would compromise the enjoyment of the work.”

29 September answer (as reviewed)
No, the deployer has no marking duty: machine-readable marking of AI-generated audio is a provider duty under Article 50(2), which falls on the provider of the generative system. A disclosure duty still applies under Article 50(4) where the audio is a deep fake, meaning it resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear authentic or truthful. Because the audio forms part of an evidently artistic or creative work, Article 50(4) limits that duty to disclosing that such generated content exists, in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work, such as a credit or accompanying note.
Citations: Article 50.4, Article 50.2Expected (official appendix): Article 50.4
26 October answer (production)
Making AI-generated audio does not by itself put a marking duty on the person who made it: the Article 50(2) duty to mark outputs in a machine-readable format and make them detectable as artificially generated belongs to the provider of the system. A person using the system is a deployer under Article 3(4) only where the use is not in the course of a personal non-professional activity, and the Act does not apply to the obligations of a natural person using an AI system in the course of a purely personal non-professional activity (Article 2(10)); a person who develops the system, or has it developed, and puts it into service for their own use under their own name or trademark is also its provider (Article 3(3) and Article 3(11)) and so bears that marking duty. Where the audio is a deep fake, meaning AI-generated or manipulated content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful (Article 3(60)), the deployer must disclose that it has been artificially generated or manipulated (Article 50(4)), and where it forms part of an evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous work that duty is limited to disclosing the existence of the generated content in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work. The information must be clear and distinguishable, given at the latest at the time of first exposure and accessible (Article 50(5)), and these duties apply from 2 August 2026.

Q95

“Area” and “use case” under Article 6(2)

Review: correct overall, 3 precision points26 Oct: 3 of 3 points resolved

“What is an 'area' and what is a 'use case' for high-risk as per Article 6(2)? How many areas exist?”

29 September answer (as reviewed)
Under Article 6(2), an AI system is high-risk where it falls within Annex III. Annex III is organised into areas, which are its numbered headings, and use cases, which are the specific lettered entries under each area. A system is high-risk only if it matches a listed use case, so belonging to an area is not enough on its own. There are eight areas: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education and vocational training, employment and workers management, access to essential private and public services and benefits, law enforcement, migration, asylum and border control management, and administration of justice and democratic processes. Under Article 7(1), the Commission may add or modify use cases only within these existing areas, and only where the risk is equivalent to or greater than that of the systems already listed.
Citations: Article 6.2, Annex III, Article 7.1Expected (official appendix): Article 6.2, Annex III
26 October answer (production)
Under Article 6(2), the AI systems referred to in Annex III are high-risk, and Annex III lists them under eight numbered area headings: biometrics; critical infrastructure; education and vocational training; employment, workers' management and access to self-employment; access to and enjoyment of essential private services and essential public services and benefits; law enforcement; migration, asylum and border control management; and administration of justice and democratic processes. An area is one of those eight numbered headings. A use case is a specific intended use listed within an area: in seven of the areas the uses are set out as lettered points, and for critical infrastructure the heading itself states the use, AI systems intended to be used as safety components in the management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, road traffic, or in the supply of water, gas, heating or electricity. Eight areas therefore exist, and a system is high-risk under Article 6(2) only where it matches a listed use case, so operating within an area is not enough on its own.

5 · Method and rephrased questions

  • Baseline. The ten answers the review assessed, with their citations, as printed in the report update of 29 September 2026.
  • Re-run. One single-turn request per question, worded as in the review, to the production endpoint on 26 October 2026 (build 51a825ec6bb0). Response times 1.5–10.3 s. Compared with 23 October, unchanged apart from punctuation in one answer.
  • Provenance. Replayed with no language model reachable, the system returned identical answers and citations for all ten questions: these are the system's statutory answers, not generated text. The language model stage (Claude Opus 5.5) was operational on the same deployment and answers questions outside the statutory answers.
  • Assessment. A point is marked addressed where the 26 October answer states it, and removed where the review objected to wording the answer no longer contains (two points). When this document is generated, quoted evidence is verified verbatim against the live answer and removed wording is verified absent. The 87 statutory passages the answers draw on were located verbatim in the Official Journal text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and, for Case D, in Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (consolidated version of 19 July 2026); the answers quote some of them and paraphrase others.
  • Scope. Single-turn answers only (the challenge's easy mode). The hard mode, nine prior turns and a challenge to the answer, was not re-run.
  • Samples for the third round. Each correction was compared before and after on the same questions and judged by Claude Sonnet 5 in both orders. Recognition: 34 independent rewordings and 14 look-alikes, routed before and after; of the 61 changed answers the judge reached 10 (the 3 from the independent set, then 7 existing questions the change moved, in file order) before it was stopped. Formatting: 182 pre-drafted answers (30 changed, all judged) and 111 model answers (11 with lists, 9 judged). Policing: 24 independent questions routed before and after, where no answer changed, and one development answer, preferred after the correction. The two corrected answers in the update are shown live and were not judged. The independent questions were written without reference to the system's code and recorded before the final version of the change they test.
  • Rephrasings prepared during the remediation. 30 rephrasings, three per question, asked on the same build. The remediation was adjusted in response to some of them, so this set is not out-of-sample. 21 were answered by the statutory answers: 15 identical to the reviewed answer, 4 the same answer worded for the question, 2 another statutory answer that fits the question, and none shortened. 9 were answered by the language model from retrieved provisions. The same 21 were answered by the statutory answers on 23 October.
  • Held-out rephrasings. 20 further rephrasings, two per question, recorded before the second round. An intermediate run of this set prompted one further change on 23 October (the area-and-use-case answer), and the seven answers it showed to be poor guided the third round, so neither later column is out-of-sample; the independent figures are at the head of this appendix. A separate model (Claude Sonnet 5) graded each answer's text against a reference position for its question family, condensed from the review's corrected answer or correct position: correct; partly correct (a material omission); incorrect (a legal error, including a fact the question does not state taken as given). The statutory answers were drafted from the same positions, so their grades are not independent; the language-model answers are the stronger test. Every answer and its citations were also checked by hand; where that differs from the judge, its grade is in brackets.

6 · Open limitations

Found by the rephrased questions and not fixed.

  • Unfamiliar rewordings still reach the language model. 28 of the 34 independent rewordings do (routing measured offline), and 6 of the 20 held-out ones (live). The statutory answers are found by recognising how a question is put, and each round of recognition fits the phrasings it was shown. The language-model answers to the held-out rewordings reach no wrong conclusion on hand check, but one makes fatigue monitoring depend on its purpose where Recital 18 does not, and one cites a provision that does not fit the question (Article 26, for an AI spam filter).
  • A statutory answer keeps its opening when the question differs. “What procedure applies when the Commission wants to update the list of high-risk use cases?” receives the Article 7 answer, which opens “Yes, but only within the limits Article 7 sets.” The procedure that follows is complete.
  • Scenario-style questions. A long question that opens with a company description (a start-up building an AI that steers the needle during robot-assisted biopsies) is handled as a scenario and so reaches the language model, not the Case D answer. It was found in the recognition tests (routing measured offline), not in a live run.
